\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Our central result is that the reduced-order fiber sampling used to
keep selectivity models tractable systematically overestimates how much
selectivity is actually delivered to the nerve, by an amount set by
fascicular architecture.  We first validate the simulator against
NEURON and benchmark its throughput
(Sections~\ref{sec:results-validation}--\ref{sec:results-scaling}),
then optimize selectivity on real cross-species anatomy
(Section~\ref{sec:results-duke}), where this central result emerges
(Section~\ref{sec:results-sparse}).  All raw data and analysis code are
available in the project repository.

\subsection{Validation against NEURON}
\label{sec:results-validation}

We first establish that jaxon reproduces NEURON across models and
scales to whole-nerve populations --- the accuracy and throughput the
optimization analysis below relies on.  For each axon model we drove
jaxon and \texttt{pyfibers}-wrapped NEURON with identical stimuli and
compared activation thresholds across diameters, eight waveforms
(monophasic and biphasic cathodic- and anodic-first, sinusoidal,
sawtooth, exponential, Gaussian) and pulse widths
$0.02$--$1\,\mathrm{ms}$ --- 943 configurations in total.
\textbf{$99.6\,\%$ agree to within $1\,\%$ relative threshold error}
(Table~\ref{tab:validation-sd}; jaxon--NEURON $r = 0.999998$, mean
signed bias $+0.11\,\%$).  Only the Rattay model exceeds the criterion,
in a single edge regime --- biphasic cathodic-first pulses at the four
smallest diameters (peak $2.24\,\%$ at $D=0.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) --- which
we attribute to reduced bisection resolution near the
strength--duration inflection; the intracellular traces agree to
NEURON-level precision (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}\textbf{a}).

Seven of the eight waveforms agree within $1\,\%$ on \emph{every}
configuration; the lone exceptions are the four biphasic cathodic-first
Rattay cases above (and even there $96.5\,\%$ pass).  Conduction
velocity, which probes the internodal and nodal time constants, agrees
to a median $<0.1\,\%$ across all models and diameters (peak $0.24\,\%$
for Sundt at $D=0.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$).  Per-waveform and per-diameter
breakdowns are in Supplementary
Tables~\ref{tab:validation-sd-per-wf}--\ref{tab:validation-cv}; the
per-waveform error distribution is Figure~\ref{fig:validation}\textbf{c}.
Beyond thresholds, jaxon reproduces three canonical propagation
phenomena --- action-potential propagation, $20\,\mathrm{kHz}$
conduction block and AP collision --- in both a myelinated and an
unmyelinated model, each matching NEURON within the scan resolution or
the floating-point floor (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:phenomena}).

Across panel c the jaxon-vs-NEURON scatter lies on the unity line at

distribution sits below the $1\,\%$ acceptance criterion.

\subsection{Forward-pass throughput}
\label{sec:results-scaling}

The principal performance motivation for the JAX implementation is
the expectation that GPU vmap-batching will outperform CPU-parallel
NEURON as the fiber count grows.  We benchmarked all five
implemented axon models (MRG with both the original and the
interpolated parameter tables, Sweeney, Sundt and Rattay) at
$N \in \{1, 10, 100, 1\,000, 10\,000, 100\,000\}$ fibers on a single
NVIDIA A100 GPU, against \texttt{pyfibers} running 8-thread
CPU-parallel on a matched Xeon host.
Figure~\ref{fig:validation}\textbf{e} reports the per-model wall time
and the resulting jaxon-vs-pyfibers speedup;
Table~\ref{tab:scaling} summarizes the headline numbers.

At small $N$, GPU launch overhead dominates and \texttt{pyfibers} on
CPU is faster, but the vmap-batched forward pass amortizes the per-step
launch cost across the batch, so jaxon overtakes \texttt{pyfibers} at
$N \approx 10$ for every model and the advantage grows with $N$
(Table~\ref{tab:scaling}).  At $N=100\,000$ the speedup reaches
$544\times$ for MRG, $1\,377\times$ for Sweeney (smaller per-fiber
state) and ${\sim}1\,000\times$ for the C-fibers, a geometric mean of
$\sim\!820\times$ --- taking what would be multiple CPU-hours per
forward pass down to under twenty seconds.  This is a GPU-versus-eight-thread-CPU
comparison against the field-standard \texttt{pyfibers} workflow; against
many-core CPU or GPU-accelerated NEURON variants the multiplier would be
smaller, but the order-of-magnitude advantage from \texttt{vmap}-batching the
forward pass persists, and CPU NEURON is the established baseline a new solver
must beat.  This is what brings the
population-scale optimize-and-re-score analysis below
($\sim\!1\,000$ fibers per nerve, simulated thousands of times per
optimization, across 29 anatomies and five sampling levels) within
reach on a single GPU-day.

\subsection{Selectivity optimization on histology-derived vagus nerves}
\label{sec:results-duke}

Using jaxon --- validated against NEURON and fast enough to optimize
whole-nerve populations (Sections~\ref{sec:results-validation}--\ref{sec:results-scaling})
--- we ran the differentiable optimizer on a cohort of
$n_{\mathrm{cohort}} = 29$ (18 swine, 11 human; 116 target-cluster
seeds) subject-specific FE-modeled vagus-nerve cross-sections drawn
from the SPARC quantified morphology releases of
swine~\cite{pelot2020swineSPARC} and human~\cite{pelot2021humanSPARC},
with per-contact extracellular lead-fields computed in-house with the
Golgi pipeline~\cite{haberbusch2026golgi}
(Section~\ref{sec:methods-duke}).  The 12-contact MultiContact cuff
(three axial rows $\times$ four angular columns) gives the rectangular
optimizer $K=12$ amplitudes per nerve; the target is the peripheral
fascicle cluster within one contiguous angular sector
(Section~\ref{sec:methods-duke}).  Figure~\ref{fig:duke}\textbf{a}
shows dense- and sparse-optimized activation maps and cuff patterns for
a representative swine and human nerve (the sparse condition is defined
in Section~\ref{sec:results-sparse}), fibers colored by activation
status (target/off-target $\times$ fired/silent).

\paragraph{Dense optimization recovers focal, selective drive.}
The rectangular optimizer uses a probe-based smart initialization
(Section~\ref{sec:methods-opt}): a small set of bipolar and
single-column tripolar spatial patterns is scored at eight magnitudes
in both polarities (80 forward passes per seed), and the best
initializes a single gradient (Adam-FD) trajectory with the probe's
zero contacts frozen to preserve sparsity.  Across the cohort ($111$ selectable-target seeds
over 29 nerves; five degenerate-target seeds excluded, see below) this
achieves mean $\mathrm{SI} = 0.90$ (median $0.95$), with $70\,\%$ of
seeds reaching $\mathrm{SI} \geq 0.90$.
Swine and human reach a near-identical median ($0.946$ vs.\ $0.964$),
but the human \emph{mean} is lower ($0.845$ vs.\ $0.926$) because a
heavy tail of geometric-ceiling anatomies (below) pulls it down rather
than a uniform downward shift.  The optimizer converges to a focal,
sparse drive: $103$ of $111$ seeds ($93\,\%$) use exactly three active
contacts --- a single-column tripolar configuration (cathode at the
column's $z$-middle contact, charge-balancing anodes at $z$-top and
$z$-bottom); per-species figures are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:duke-cohort}.  Per-sample dense and
sparse-transfer activation maps with their cuff
patterns, for every nerve and seed, are provided as Supplementary
Figures~\ref{fig:supp-sub-4-sam-3}--\ref{fig:supp-human-sub-58-sam-3}.
All results here use the fixed charge-balanced biphasic pulse with
optimized per-contact amplitudes; waveform-shape optimization is left
to future work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Emergent stimulation strategy.}
The optimized configurations are physically interpretable rather than
arbitrary (Figure~\ref{fig:duke}\textbf{c}).  Circumferentially, the
optimizer steers cathodic drive \emph{toward} the target sector: the
fraction of contacts that are cathodic is highest in the
target-facing direction ($0.34$ swine, $0.40$ human) and near zero to
the side.  Longitudinally it forms a center--surround current profile
--- a mid-cathode tripole flanked by charge-balancing anodes ---
whose polarity is a species-dependent tendency (more often the
classic mid-cathode form in swine, the inverse in human).  The
optimizer thus rediscovers, without being told to, the steered
tripolar configurations that the selectivity literature arrives at by
design.

\paragraph{Geometric-ceiling cases.}
A minority of human anatomies cannot be steered well.  These are not
primarily cases where off-target fascicles happen to sit as close to the
contacts as the target --- across the cohort the minimum
fascicle-to-contact distance is only a weak predictor of achieved
selectivity (Spearman $r \approx -0.13$, n.s.) --- but cases where the
target fascicle is large relative to the steerable field, so that sparing
every off-target fiber while recruiting the whole target is geometrically
impossible.  These geometric-ceiling cases form the heavy lower tail of
the human distribution and are \emph{retained} in all statistics ---
they are genuine selectivity outcomes on selectable targets, not solver
artifacts.  Separately, we exclude five seeds (all human) whose target
cluster spans more than $60\,\%$ of the nerve cross-section (target
fraction $> 0.6$): such targets are degenerate by construction --- no
selective solution exists --- so they are uninformative about achievable
selectivity.

\subsection{Reduced-order fiber sampling overestimates deliverable
  selectivity unless the design is re-scored on the population}
\label{sec:results-sparse}

Prior GPU-accelerated selectivity work reduces each fascicle to a
single centroid fiber to keep the population
tractable~\cite{hussain2024axonml}.  Because jaxon simulates the full
${\sim}1000$-fiber population directly, we can ask what this
approximation costs at deployment.  For each nerve $\times$ seed we
re-ran the identical optimization on four sparse fiber-sampling levels
--- one centroid fiber per fascicle, and $1$, $3$ or $10$
randomly-sampled fibers per fascicle --- by pure row-subsampling of the
precomputed $V_e$ lead-field with no FEM recomputation.  The
selectivity such a model \emph{reports} is optimistic, because its
amplitudes are tuned to the few fibers it can see; we therefore take
each sparse-optimized amplitude vector and re-score it on the full
population \emph{without re-optimizing}, giving the transfer
selectivity $\mathrm{SI_{transfer}}$ --- the selectivity actually
delivered.  The deployment penalty is the drop from the dense ceiling,
$\mathrm{SI_{dense}} - \mathrm{SI_{transfer}}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:duke}\textbf{d}).

The penalty is negligible in swine but substantial and heavy-tailed in
human.  For the single-representative-fiber reduction that reduced-order
pipelines actually use --- one fiber at each fascicle centroid --- the
per-nerve penalty is a median $0.011$ in swine (mean $0.013$, maximum
$0.05$) versus $0.136$ in human (mean $0.128$; Mann--Whitney
$p = 0.01$), against a dense ceiling of ${\sim}0.95$.  The human
distribution is right-skewed: nearly half of the human nerves lose more
than $0.10$ and the worst loses $0.45$, whereas no swine nerve loses more
than $0.05$.  Sampling \emph{more} fibers reduces the penalty --- across
the four strategies it falls significantly as density rises (one to ten
random fibers per target fascicle, median $0.19 \rightarrow 0.03$;
Friedman $p = 0.002$; averaged over strategies the human penalty is
$0.083$) --- but it does not close on the largest fascicles, where three
of eleven human nerves still lose more than $0.10$ even at ten fibers
(Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:density}).  Re-expressed
as the fraction of each target fascicle sampled --- the species-fair
axis, since ten fibers is ${\sim}33\,\%$ of a typical swine fascicle but
only ${\sim}7\,\%$ of a human one --- the penalty concentrates below
${\sim}5\,\%$ sampling and the species gap persists at matched low
fraction (human ${\sim}0.10$ vs swine ${\sim}0.00$): a large fascicle
needs a proportionally larger sample to be represented, and on the human
vagus the standard single-fiber reduction rarely provides it.

What sets the species difference is fascicle \emph{size}.  The penalty is
essentially a between-species step --- negligible in swine, sizeable in
human --- tracking the number of fibers per target fascicle, which has
median $30$ in swine versus $151$ in human.  The strategy-averaged penalty
correlates with fibers-per-fascicle across the cohort (Spearman
$r = +0.47$, $p = 0.01$, $n = 29$ nerves), though within each species the
trend is only directional at these sample sizes; we therefore read
fibers-per-fascicle as a \emph{between-species} driver rather than a
graded within-species predictor.
This explains the species gap: swine vagus nerves carry
many small fascicles, so a handful of sampled fibers represents the
target well and the optimized field transfers; human nerves carry a few
large fascicles, so a field tuned to one sampled fiber does not cover
the rest of that fascicle.  This is the within-fascicle counterpart of
an established geometric effect --- cuff-electrode activation thresholds
vary systematically with a fascicle's perineurium thickness and
cross-sectional area~\cite{davis2023fascicle} --- so within one large
fascicle the spread of fiber positions produces a spread of thresholds
that a single centroid fiber cannot represent.  Counterintuitively, the
more fascicular nerve (swine) is the more forgiving one.

The recruitment signature confirms the mechanism
(Figure~\ref{fig:duke}\textbf{b}).  Relative to the dense optimum, the
centroid-transfer configuration in human loses on-target recruitment
(median $100\,\% \rightarrow 83\,\%$; $p = 0.004$) and selectivity
($p = 0.002$), whereas off-target recruitment and total delivered
current are statistically unchanged ($p > 0.2$).  The penalty is thus a
failure to recruit the large target fascicle, not off-target spill or
reduced drive.  It is, however, largely a cost of the \emph{standard
workflow} --- optimizing on the reduced set and reading selectivity off
it, without re-scoring on the population.  Because jaxon re-scores a
candidate on the full population cheaply, selecting per nerve the
sparse-optimized design that scores best on the full population
(optimize-then-validate) collapses the penalty to a median $0.000$ in
swine and $0.016$ in human.  The honest conclusion is therefore narrow:
centroid-only sampling \emph{as standardly used}~\cite{hussain2024axonml}
over-states deliverable selectivity on the large-fascicle human anatomies
most relevant to clinical translation, but a practitioner who brings the
full population into the loop --- at optimization or at least at
validation, now computationally feasible --- recovers most of it, leaving
only a small residual (${\sim}0.02$ in human) that no sparse design fully
closes.
