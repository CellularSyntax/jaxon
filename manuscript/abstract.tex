Computational models guide the design of selective peripheral nerve
stimulation, but to remain tractable they almost universally reduce
each fascicle to one or a few representative fibers.  Whether this
reduction biases the selectivity it predicts has been untestable,
because testing it requires simulating entire fiber populations under
optimized extracellular fields.  We built \emph{jaxon}, a fully
differentiable, GPU-native reimplementation in JAX of the canonical
peripheral nerve fiber models --- the myelinated
McIntyre--Richardson--Grill and Sweeney axons and the unmyelinated
Sundt and Rattay C-fibers --- validated against
\texttt{pyfibers}-wrapped NEURON (\textbf{$99.6\,\%$ of 943
configurations within $1\,\%$ threshold error}) and reaching a
\textbf{geometric-mean $\sim\!820\times$ speedup} at $N = 100{,}000$
fibers on one NVIDIA A100, fast enough to optimize and re-score
whole-nerve populations.  Using FEM-derived fields over a 12-contact
cuff on 29 microCT-segmented vagus nerves (18 swine, 11 human; SPARC),
we find that configurations optimized on sparsely-sampled fibers lose
substantial selectivity when deployed on the full population --- a
\emph{deployment penalty} invisible to reduced-order models.  The
penalty is small in swine (median $0.03$) but large in human ($0.27$)
and scales with the number of fibers per target fascicle (Spearman
$r = +0.72$), which differs systematically between species; it
manifests as failure to recruit the large human target fascicle rather
than off-target spill.  Reduced-order fascicular sampling therefore
overstates the selectivity that will actually be delivered, most
severely on the human vagus geometry most relevant to clinical
translation.  jaxon is open-source and slots into existing pipelines as
a gradient-enabled replacement for the NEURON forward solver.
