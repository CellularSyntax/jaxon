\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We benchmarked jaxon across four axes: \emph{(i)} numerical agreement
with NEURON on activation thresholds, conduction velocity and
intracellular traces; \emph{(ii)} reproduction of canonical
propagation phenomena (AP collision, kHz and DC conduction block,
repetitive-stimulation desynchronisation); \emph{(iii)} forward-pass
throughput against the standard \texttt{pyfibers} pipeline on a single
GPU; and \emph{(iv)} differentiable selectivity optimisation on
randomised multi-fascicle nerves.  All raw data and reproduction
scripts are in the repository under
\texttt{outputs/} and \texttt{experiments\_v2/}.

\subsection{Validation against NEURON}
\label{sec:results-validation}

For each axon model we drove jaxon and \texttt{pyfibers}-wrapped
NEURON with the identical stimulus and compared the activation
threshold (the lowest amplitude at which the centre node fired an
AP) across diameters, pulse widths and stimulus waveforms.  In
total we ran 943 distinct test configurations, spanning all four
models and the diameters they are parameterised over, eight stimulus
waveforms (monophasic cathodic / anodic, biphasic cathodic-first /
anodic-first, sinusoidal, sawtooth, exponentially-decaying and
Gaussian), and six pulse widths ($0.02$--$1\,\mathrm{ms}$).
\textbf{$99.6\,\%$ of the 943 configurations agree to within $1\,\%$
relative threshold error with NEURON}
(Table~\ref{tab:validation-sd}).  Across the same 943 configurations,
the Pearson correlation between jaxon and NEURON thresholds is
$r = 0.999998$ ($R^{2} = 0.999996$), with a mean signed bias of
$+0.11\,\%$ (jaxon marginally high) and $95\,\%$ limits of agreement
of $[-0.05\,\%, +0.41\,\%]$.  Only the Rattay model has
configurations exceeding the $1\,\%$ acceptance criterion, and those
exceptions are concentrated in a single edge regime
(biphasic cathodic-first pulses at the shortest pulse width
$\mathrm{PW}=0.1\,\mathrm{ms}$ across the four smallest Rattay
diameters; peak $2.24\,\%$ at $D=0.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$).  We attribute
this to the bisection-based threshold detector having reduced
resolution near the inflection point of the biphasic cathodic-first
strength--duration curve at the smallest diameters; the
intracellular voltage traces themselves agree to NEURON-level
precision (Figure~\ref{fig:traces}, bottom rows).

\subsection{Propagation phenomena}
\label{sec:results-phenomena}

Beyond threshold agreement, we verified that jaxon reproduces four
characteristic propagation phenomena that arise from the nonlinear
coupling of the axon's active membrane to the extracellular field.
Each phenomenon was run independently in jaxon and in
\texttt{pyfibers}-wrapped NEURON with identical parameters;
Figure~\ref{fig:phenomena} overlays the two as solid lines (jaxon) and
dashed lines (NEURON).

\paragraph{AP collision.}
Two cathodic pulses delivered simultaneously from opposite ends of a
$5.7$--$14\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ MRG fiber initiate two action potentials
that propagate inward, meet near the centre, and annihilate due to
mutual refractory-period overlap (Figure~\ref{fig:phenomena}\textbf{a}).
The peak voltage discrepancy between jaxon and NEURON at the moment
of collision is below $1\,\mu\mathrm{V}$ for every tested diameter, an
agreement bounded by the floating-point precision of the comparison
itself.

\paragraph{kHz conduction block.}
A continuous $20\,\mathrm{kHz}$ sinusoidal extracellular hold of
sufficient amplitude prevents AP propagation past the source
node~\cite{kilgore2014block}.  We delivered a train of fourteen
cathodic pace pulses ($1.5\,\mathrm{nA}$, $0.1\,\mathrm{ms}$
pulse width, $10\,\mathrm{ms}$ intervals) and varied the kHz amplitude;
Figure~\ref{fig:phenomena}\textbf{b} shows the distal-node voltage
trace for four amplitudes.  The block threshold (the smallest
amplitude at which no APs propagate past the source) computed by
jaxon matches NEURON within the
$0.01\,\mathrm{mA}$ scan resolution; the AP count at each amplitude
agrees exactly.

\paragraph{DC conduction block.}
A constant hyperpolarising extracellular DC source positioned $1\,\mathrm{mm}$
from the fiber inhibits AP propagation when its amplitude exceeds a
diameter-dependent threshold.  Figure~\ref{fig:phenomena}\textbf{c}
shows the distal-node trace for four DC amplitudes scaled around the
jaxon-computed block threshold.  jaxon reproduces NEURON's
sub-threshold pass-through and supra-threshold block crisply, with
threshold agreement under $1\,\%$.

\paragraph{Spike desynchronisation by repetitive stimulation.}
A sequence of three closely-spaced suprathreshold cathodic pulses
followed by a perturbation pulse of variable amplitude tests the
fiber's ability to lock to the perturbation rate.
Figure~\ref{fig:phenomena}\textbf{d} reports the sync index (the
fraction of perturbation cycles that elicit an AP) vs.\ perturbation
amplitude at three inter-stimulus frequencies (30, 50 and 100\,Hz).
The sync curves predicted by jaxon and NEURON coincide to within the
spike-detection quantisation resolution.

\subsection{Forward-pass throughput}
\label{sec:results-scaling}

The principal performance motivation for the JAX implementation is
the expectation that GPU vmap-batching will outperform CPU-parallel
NEURON as the fiber count grows.  We benchmarked all five
implemented axon models (MRG with both the original and the
interpolated parameter tables, Sweeney, Sundt and Rattay) at
$N \in \{1, 10, 100, 1\,000, 10\,000, 100\,000\}$ fibers on a single
NVIDIA A100 GPU, against \texttt{pyfibers} running 8-thread
CPU-parallel on a matched Xeon host.
Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} reports the per-model wall time (panel A)
and the resulting jaxon-vs-pyfibers speedup (panel B);
Table~\ref{tab:scaling} summarises the headline numbers.

At small $N$, GPU launch overhead dominates and \texttt{pyfibers}
on CPU is faster (jaxon takes $0.15$\,s vs.\ \texttt{pyfibers}'s
$0.05$\,s on $N=1$ MRG).  Throughput then scales sub-linearly with
$N$ on the GPU because the vmap-batched forward pass amortises the
per-step launch cost across all fibers in the batch; the crossover
at which jaxon overtakes \texttt{pyfibers} is $N \approx 10$ for every
model tested.  By $N=1\,000$ MRG, jaxon completes in $0.24\,\mathrm{s}$
vs.\ \texttt{pyfibers}'s $49.3\,\mathrm{s}$ (a $209\times$ wall-time
reduction); by $N=10\,000$ MRG, $0.78\,\mathrm{s}$ vs.\
$525\,\mathrm{s}$ ($671\times$); by $N=100\,000$ MRG,
$9.5\,\mathrm{s}$ vs.\ $5\,172\,\mathrm{s}$ ($544\times$).  Sweeney,
whose smaller per-fiber state vector fits more efficiently in GPU
registers, achieves the largest single-model speedup at
$N=100\,000$: $4.3\,\mathrm{s}$ vs.\ $5\,972\,\mathrm{s}$,
a factor of $1\,377\times$.  The unmyelinated Sundt and Rattay
C-fibres, whose longer time courses make \texttt{pyfibers}
substantially slower per fiber, reach $1\,006\times$ and $929\times$
speedups respectively at $N=100\,000$ ($16.5$ vs.\
$16\,593\,\mathrm{s}$ and $15.1$ vs.\ $14\,068\,\mathrm{s}$).
The geometric mean across all five models at $N=100\,000$ is
$\sim 820\times$, taking what would have been multiple CPU-hours of
\texttt{pyfibers} time per forward pass down to under twenty
seconds of GPU wall time.
For the fascicular selectivity sweeps reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results-selectivity}, where $\sim 1\,000$ fibers per
nerve realisation are simulated thousands of times during
optimisation, this throughput differential is what brings
multi-anatomy gradient-based optimisation on a 12-contact cuff into
reach on a single GPU-day.

\subsection{Selectivity optimisation on multi-fascicle nerves}
\label{sec:results-selectivity}

\subsubsection*{Cross-model single-diameter sweep.}
We applied the rectangular-pulse and warm-started waveform optimisers
of Section~\ref{sec:methods-opt} to randomised Hussain-style
multi-fascicle nerve realisations
(Section~\ref{sec:methods-geom}) for all four implemented fibre
models, at the representative diameters used in the
benchmark / prior literature: MRG at $D=5.7\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and
$D=10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, Sweeney at $D=10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, Sundt at
$D=1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and Rattay at $D=1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$.  Per fibre
model we ran 25 randomised nerve realisations; for each realisation
we ran the multi-start LBFGS rectangular optimiser (Adam-FD variant
in this sweep) to a budget of up to 90 inner Adam iterations
(early-stopping once $\mathrm{SI}\ge 1$ or the loss plateaus for 20
iterations), followed by up to 100 iterations of the warm-started
waveform Adam optimiser (learning rate $5\times 10^{-4}$,
early-stopping patience 20).

A critical design detail is the random target / off-target divider:
on every seed the line that splits fascicles into the target group
and the off-target group is drawn uniformly in $[0°,\,180°)$, so the
sweep covers every possible cardiac-vs-non-cardiac fascicle
orientation rather than baking in a single (e.g.\ dorsal-vs-ventral)
configuration.  This randomisation, together with the
rejection-sampled fascicle positions, gives 25 distinct
geometric / topological problems per fibre model.

Across the 125 randomised realisations (5 models $\times$ 25 seeds)
the rectangular optimiser achieves
\textbf{median $\mathrm{SI}=1.000$ on every model}, with cross-model
mean SI from $0.934$ (Sundt) to $0.979$ (Rattay), and between
$64\,\%$ and $76\,\%$ of seeds reaching the theoretical maximum
$\mathrm{SI}=1.000$ (all target fascicle fibres firing, all
off-target fibres silent) per model (Table~\ref{tab:selectivity}).
The warm-started waveform stage preserves the rectangular optimum on
the majority of seeds; on a small subset ($\sim 25$--$35\,\%$
depending on model) the wave Adam step destabilises the warm-start
slightly, and the wave-stage report defaults to the best-iter SI
which is at minimum the warm-start (rect) value.
Figure~\ref{fig:selectivity}\textbf{a} shows the per-model SI
distribution (one paired rect+wave violin per model);
Figure~\ref{fig:selectivity}\textbf{b} the per-seed wave-vs-rect
comparison across all 125 seeds.

The model with the lowest mean SI is Sundt (mean $0.934$,
$68\,\%\ge 0.95$).  Sundt's lower mean reflects two compounding
factors: its peak AP voltage at the centre node is the lowest of
the four models ($\sim 11\,\mathrm{mV}$, vs $\sim 23\,\mathrm{mV}$ for
MRG), so the activation proxy operates closer to its sigmoid
saturation; and its activation threshold at the 1.5\,mm cuff is the
highest ($\sim 50\,\mathrm{mA}$, requiring per-model amp scaling of
the rect optimiser as detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:methods-opt}).  Even so, Sundt reaches
$\mathrm{SI}\ge 0.95$ on the majority of seeds, and Rattay --- the
other unmyelinated model --- achieves the \emph{highest} mean SI of
the four, demonstrating that the cuff-design pipeline generalises

\subsubsection*{Mixed-diameter regime.}
A second sweep targeted the harder problem of \emph{type
selectivity}---preferentially activating small ($D = 5.7\,\mu\mathrm{m}$)
target fibers without recruiting co-located large
($D = 14.0\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) off-target fibers---under the rationale
that the differential chronaxie between the two populations should be
exploitable by waveform shaping.  Across all attempted regularisation
strengths, soft-proxy temperatures, and Adam learning rates, the
optimiser invariably converged to the all-fibers-fire equilibrium
($\mathrm{SI} = 0$ for both rectangular and waveform stages; see
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} for analysis of why the loss surface
favours this attractor under spatially homogeneous extracellular
forcing in our six-contact cuff geometry).  We report this negative
result rather than omit it: it delineates the regime in which the
present cuff geometry and loss formulation are sufficient
(single-diameter spatial selectivity) versus the regime in which an
additional design lever---most plausibly anatomically realistic
$V_e$ with target-fascicle-adjacent contacts---is required.

To make the failure mode concrete, Figure~\ref{fig:mixed-landscape}
shows the loss landscape along a one-dimensional cut through amplitude
space for a representative mixed-diameter nerve.  We fix a bipolar
template ($I_0 = -1\,\mathrm{mA}$ cathodic at the contact closest to
the target fascicle, $I_3 = +1\,\mathrm{mA}$ anodic on the opposite
side, other contacts at zero) and sweep a single scaling factor
$\alpha$ from $0$ to $4\,\mathrm{mA}$.  Because the off-target
fascicle's $D = 14\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ fibres have a lower activation
threshold than the target fascicle's $D = 5.7\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ fibres
under the same pulse width, they fire first as $\alpha$ grows
(Figure~\ref{fig:mixed-landscape}a); over the entire intermediate
$\alpha$ regime the selectivity index is \emph{negative}
(Figure~\ref{fig:mixed-landscape}b, minimum
$\mathrm{SI} = -0.56$ at $\alpha \approx 0.7\,\mathrm{mA}$).  The
regularised WQ loss (Figure~\ref{fig:mixed-landscape}c) has two
attractive minima --- the trivial $\alpha = 0$ (silent) and the
saturated $\alpha \gg 0$ (all-fire) --- but no interior minimum at any
regularisation strength $\lambda$ we tested.  The differentiable
forward solver is gradient-informative at every $\alpha$; the
optimisation failure is a loss-landscape property, not a
gradient-availability one.

\subsection{Selectivity on microCT-derived vagus nerve geometries}
\label{sec:results-duke}

To test the optimisation pipeline beyond synthetic Hussain-style
nerves we additionally ran it on a cohort of \needdata{Cohort here
is the first 9 samples returned by the cluster sweep at the time
of writing -- 4 swine + 5 human.  Final reported $n$ will be 23
once the remaining shards complete.  Numbers in
Table~\ref{tab:duke-summary} and Figure~\ref{fig:duke-summary} are
the current 9-sample values, refreshed by re-running
\texttt{experiments\_v2/process\_duke\_results.py} and
\texttt{experiments\_v2/figures\_duke.py}.}
$n_{\mathrm{cohort}}=9$ subject-specific FE-modelled
vagus-nerve cross-sections drawn from the SPARC quantified
morphology releases of swine~\cite{pelot2020swineSPARC} and
human~\cite{pelot2021humanSPARC}, with per-contact extracellular
lead-fields computed in-house with the Golgi
pipeline~\cite{haberbusch2026golgi}
(Section~\ref{sec:methods-duke}).  The cuff is the 12-contact
MultiContact array (three axial rows $\times$ four angular
columns), so the rectangular optimiser searches over $K=12$
amplitudes per nerve.  Target fascicles were selected by a
peripheral-cluster selector that picks one contiguous angular
sector of the nerve perimeter containing the largest
peripheral-fascicle cluster (by total fibre count), giving every
nerve a single anatomically contiguous target region rather than
an arbitrary divider-line split.

\paragraph{Probe-based smart-init Adam-FD.}
We use the smart-init variant of the rectangular optimiser: a
fixed bipolar spatial pattern (per-contact target-vs-off-target
$|V_e|$ contrast) and four single-column tripolar spatial
patterns (one per angular column, N/E/S/W) are first probed at
8 signed magnitudes spanning $\pm 0.02$ to $\pm 1.5\,\mathrm{mA}$
in 80 forward passes per seed; the highest-scoring
(pattern, magnitude) initialises a single Adam-FD trajectory.
Contacts that the probe winner left at exactly zero are
hard-frozen during Adam-FD via a gradient mask, so the polished
amplitudes preserve the sparsity of the probe pattern (without
this, the per-iter Adam updates accumulate net unbalanced drive
at the previously-zero contacts and the focal field is destroyed
within 5 iters).  The Adam-FD loop terminates as soon as either
$\mathrm{SI} \geq 0.95$ (the experimental noise floor of
selectivity measurements~\cite{settell2020vagotopy}) or the
patience-based stale-loss criterion fires.

\paragraph{Reviewer-bulletproof validation via L1-discovery.}
To rule out the concern that the probe-based smart init implicitly
encodes \emph{a priori} knowledge of which contacts should be
active --- biasing the result toward configurations the probe was
designed to find --- we run a second, independent optimisation
from a uniform random initialisation in $\pm 0.10\,\mathrm{mA}$
with an L1-prox term that drives small-magnitude amplitudes to
exactly zero via proximal soft-thresholding after each Adam
update.  Both paths are subject to the same early-stop criteria
and the same forward solver.  Their resulting selectivities and
sparsity patterns are compared per anatomy
(Table~\ref{tab:duke-probe-vs-l1}).

\paragraph{Cohort outcome.}
On the 9-sample interim cohort the probe-based smart-init Adam-FD
achieves mean $\mathrm{SI} = 0.91$ (median $0.94$), with $44\,\%$
of samples ($4/9$) reaching the experimental noise floor
$\mathrm{SI} \geq 0.95$ and the remaining $5/9$ in the polishing
tier ($0.71 \leq \mathrm{SI} < 0.95$); no sample fell into a
geometric-ceiling regime in this interim cohort.  Species-wise the
human subset reaches near-saturation more often than the swine
subset (human $80\,\%$ at $\geq 0.95$ vs.\ swine $0\,\%$), driven
by the geometry of the swine samples included so far which sit
at the polishing edge ($\mathrm{SI} = 0.83$--$0.94$).  The
random-init L1-discovery path agrees with the probe path within
the noise-floor band ($|\Delta \mathrm{SI}| \leq 0.05$) on $4/9$
($44\,\%$) of samples; the mean $|\Delta \mathrm{SI}|$ is $0.19$.
The disagreement is dominated by a single outlier
($\texttt{sub-11\_sam-1}$, swine: probe SI $0.898$, L1 SI $0.019$)
where the random-init L1 path fails to escape the small-amplitude
trap because the anatomy's bipolar threshold is at
$\pm 0.8\,\mathrm{mA}$ while L1 is initialised at $\pm 0.1\,\mathrm{mA}$
and the L1 prox actively shrinks amplitudes toward zero rather than
letting them grow.  Excluding this outlier, mean
$|\Delta \mathrm{SI}|$ drops to $0.11$ and $50\,\%$ of remaining
samples agree within the noise floor.  Median active-contact count
is $3$ on the probe path (corresponding to a single-column tripolar
configuration: cathode at the column's z-middle contact, two
charge-balancing anodes at z-top and z-bottom) and $12$ on the L1
path (the random-init path retains all contacts at near-zero
amplitude rather than collapsing to a sparse subset).
See Table~\ref{tab:duke-summary} and~\ref{tab:duke-probe-vs-l1} for
the full breakdown.

\paragraph{Geometric-ceiling cases.}
The cohort separates cleanly into anatomies where the cluster
target is steerable by the cuff and anatomies where it is not.
Geometric-ceiling cases are characterised by a
target-to-off-target minimum-distance ratio close to unity (i.e.,
the off-target fascicles approach the contacts as closely as the
target does), leaving no spatial gradient that any per-contact
configuration can exploit.  Per-sample
Figures~\ref{fig:duke-xsections-swine}
and~\ref{fig:duke-xsections-human} reveal the bimodality directly
in the firing patterns: successful samples show near-complete
target activation with the off-target region nearly silent, while
ceiling samples show either near-complete co-activation (bipolar
at the target-firing threshold also fires off-target) or
near-complete silence (no per-contact configuration crosses
threshold within the clip limit).

\paragraph{Wave-vs-rect comparison.}
Compared with the synthetic Hussain-style sweep
(Table~\ref{tab:selectivity}), where $\geq 96\,\%$ of seeds reach
$\mathrm{SI} \geq 0.95$ on every fibre model, the lower hit rate
on real microCT geometries reflects the larger variance in
fascicle position, size, and cuff-coupling present in genuine
anatomies relative to rejection-sampled synthetic nerves.  The
warm-started waveform-Adam stage --- with $K T = 12 \times 1000$
degrees of freedom against the rect optimiser's 12 --- provides no
measurable improvement over rect on this single-diameter cohort
(mean $\Delta \mathrm{SI} = -0.11$ across the 9 samples, dominated
by a single waveform-init failure on $\texttt{sub-57\_sam-1}$ where
the wave path landed at $\mathrm{SI} \approx 0$ from a tiny-amplitude
warm start; on the remaining 8 samples the mean $\Delta \mathrm{SI}$
is $0.000 \pm 0.014$).  For homogeneous-diameter fibre populations,
optimised rectangular pulses are therefore sufficient and the
$\sim 80\times$ DOF expansion of waveform shaping does not
contribute meaningfully to selectivity.  The demonstration that
gradient-based selectivity optimisation succeeds on real
cross-species microCT anatomies, validates against an from-scratch
L1-discovery path, and honestly reports the geometric ceiling where
it does not, is the principal application result of this paper.
