\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We benchmarked jaxon across four axes: \emph{(i)} numerical agreement
with NEURON on activation thresholds, conduction velocity and
intracellular traces; \emph{(ii)} reproduction of canonical
propagation phenomena (AP propagation, kHz conduction block and AP
collision) in both a myelinated and an unmyelinated fibre model;
\emph{(iii)} forward-pass throughput against the standard
\texttt{pyfibers} pipeline on a single GPU; and \emph{(iv)}
differentiable selectivity optimisation on microCT-derived vagus-nerve
anatomies.  All raw data and reproduction scripts are in the
repository under \texttt{outputs/} and \texttt{experiments\_v2/}.

\subsection{Validation against NEURON}
\label{sec:results-validation}

For each axon model we drove jaxon and \texttt{pyfibers}-wrapped
NEURON with the identical stimulus and compared the activation
threshold (the lowest amplitude at which the centre node fired an
AP) across diameters, pulse widths and stimulus waveforms.  In
total we ran 943 distinct test configurations, spanning all four
models and the diameters they are parameterised over, eight stimulus
waveforms (monophasic cathodic / anodic, biphasic cathodic-first /
anodic-first, sinusoidal, sawtooth, exponentially-decaying and
Gaussian), and six pulse widths ($0.02$--$1\,\mathrm{ms}$).
\textbf{$99.6\,\%$ of the 943 configurations agree to within $1\,\%$
relative threshold error with NEURON}
(Table~\ref{tab:validation-sd}).  Across the same 943 configurations,
the Pearson correlation between jaxon and NEURON thresholds is
$r = 0.999998$ ($R^{2} = 0.999996$), with a mean signed bias of
$+0.11\,\%$ (jaxon marginally high) and $95\,\%$ limits of agreement
of $[-0.05\,\%, +0.41\,\%]$.  Only the Rattay model has
configurations exceeding the $1\,\%$ acceptance criterion, and those
exceptions are concentrated in a single edge regime
(biphasic cathodic-first pulses at the shortest pulse width
$\mathrm{PW}=0.1\,\mathrm{ms}$ across the four smallest Rattay
diameters; peak $2.24\,\%$ at $D=0.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$).  We attribute
this to the bisection-based threshold detector having reduced
resolution near the inflection point of the biphasic cathodic-first
strength--duration curve at the smallest diameters; the
intracellular voltage traces themselves agree to NEURON-level
precision (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}\textbf{a}).

\subsection{Propagation phenomena}
\label{sec:results-phenomena}

Beyond threshold agreement, we verified that jaxon reproduces three
characteristic propagation phenomena that arise from the nonlinear
coupling of the axon's active membrane to the extracellular field, in
both a myelinated (MRG, A-fibre) and an unmyelinated (Sundt, C-fibre)
model.  Each phenomenon was run independently in jaxon and in
\texttt{pyfibers}-wrapped NEURON with identical parameters;
Figure~\ref{fig:phenomena} overlays jaxon (solid) and NEURON (dashed)
for the A-fibre (top row, \textbf{a}--\textbf{c}) and the C-fibre
(bottom row, \textbf{d}--\textbf{f}).

\paragraph{AP propagation.}
A single suprathreshold cathodic pulse delivered at the centre of the
fiber initiates an action potential that propagates symmetrically
toward both ends.  The waterfall plot
(Figure~\ref{fig:phenomena}\textbf{a},\textbf{d}) stacks the
membrane-potential trace of every node along the fiber against time,
so the diagonal sweep of the depolarisation front directly visualises
the propagation velocity.  jaxon and PyFibers traces are
visually indistinguishable at every node, for both the fast
myelinated A-fibre and the slow unmyelinated C-fibre.

\paragraph{kHz conduction block.}
A continuous $20\,\mathrm{kHz}$ sinusoidal extracellular hold of
sufficient amplitude prevents AP propagation past the source
node~\cite{kilgore2014block}.  We delivered a train of cathodic pace
pulses and varied the kHz amplitude;
Figure~\ref{fig:phenomena}\textbf{b},\textbf{e} shows the
distal-node voltage trace for four amplitudes (kHz-on window shaded),
spanning no block, partial block and full block.  The block threshold
(the smallest amplitude at which no APs propagate past the source)
computed by jaxon matches NEURON within the $0.01\,\mathrm{mA}$ scan
resolution, and the AP count at each amplitude agrees exactly, for
both fibre models.

\paragraph{AP collision.}
Two cathodic pulses delivered simultaneously from opposite ends of a
fiber initiate two action potentials that propagate inward, meet near
the centre, and annihilate due to mutual refractory-period overlap
(Figure~\ref{fig:phenomena}\textbf{c},\textbf{f}; $V_m$ along the
fiber at five times, for four diameters).  The peak voltage
discrepancy between jaxon and NEURON at the moment of collision is
below $1\,\mu\mathrm{V}$ for every tested diameter, an agreement
bounded by the floating-point precision of the comparison itself.

\subsection{Forward-pass throughput}
\label{sec:results-scaling}

The principal performance motivation for the JAX implementation is
the expectation that GPU vmap-batching will outperform CPU-parallel
NEURON as the fiber count grows.  We benchmarked all five
implemented axon models (MRG with both the original and the
interpolated parameter tables, Sweeney, Sundt and Rattay) at
$N \in \{1, 10, 100, 1\,000, 10\,000, 100\,000\}$ fibers on a single
NVIDIA A100 GPU, against \texttt{pyfibers} running 8-thread
CPU-parallel on a matched Xeon host.
Figure~\ref{fig:validation}\textbf{e} reports the per-model wall time
and the resulting jaxon-vs-pyfibers speedup;
Table~\ref{tab:scaling} summarises the headline numbers.

At small $N$, GPU launch overhead dominates and \texttt{pyfibers}
on CPU is faster (jaxon takes $0.15$\,s vs.\ \texttt{pyfibers}'s
$0.05$\,s on $N=1$ MRG).  Throughput then scales sub-linearly with
$N$ on the GPU because the vmap-batched forward pass amortises the
per-step launch cost across all fibers in the batch; the crossover
at which jaxon overtakes \texttt{pyfibers} is $N \approx 10$ for every
model tested.  By $N=1\,000$ MRG, jaxon completes in $0.24\,\mathrm{s}$
vs.\ \texttt{pyfibers}'s $49.3\,\mathrm{s}$ (a $209\times$ wall-time
reduction); by $N=10\,000$ MRG, $0.78\,\mathrm{s}$ vs.\
$525\,\mathrm{s}$ ($671\times$); by $N=100\,000$ MRG,
$9.5\,\mathrm{s}$ vs.\ $5\,172\,\mathrm{s}$ ($544\times$).  Sweeney,
whose smaller per-fiber state vector fits more efficiently in GPU
registers, achieves the largest single-model speedup at
$N=100\,000$: $4.3\,\mathrm{s}$ vs.\ $5\,972\,\mathrm{s}$,
a factor of $1\,377\times$.  The unmyelinated Sundt and Rattay
C-fibres, whose longer time courses make \texttt{pyfibers}
substantially slower per fiber, reach $1\,006\times$ and $929\times$
speedups respectively at $N=100\,000$ ($16.5$ vs.\
$16\,593\,\mathrm{s}$ and $15.1$ vs.\ $14\,068\,\mathrm{s}$).
The geometric mean across all five models at $N=100\,000$ is
$\sim 820\times$, taking what would have been multiple CPU-hours of
\texttt{pyfibers} time per forward pass down to under twenty
seconds of GPU wall time.
For the fascicular selectivity sweeps reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results-duke}, where $\sim 1\,000$ fibers per
nerve realisation are simulated thousands of times during
optimisation, this throughput differential is what brings
multi-anatomy gradient-based optimisation on a 12-contact cuff into
reach on a single GPU-day.

\subsection{Selectivity on microCT-derived vagus nerve geometries}
\label{sec:results-duke}

To test the optimisation pipeline on realistic anatomy we ran it on a
cohort of $n_{\mathrm{cohort}} = 29$ (18 swine, 11 human; 116
target-cluster seeds)
subject-specific FE-modelled vagus-nerve cross-sections drawn from the
SPARC quantified morphology releases of
swine~\cite{pelot2020swineSPARC} and human~\cite{pelot2021humanSPARC},
with per-contact extracellular lead-fields computed in-house with the
Golgi pipeline~\cite{haberbusch2026golgi}
(Section~\ref{sec:methods-duke}).  The cuff is the 12-contact
MultiContact array (three axial rows $\times$ four angular
columns), so the rectangular optimiser searches over $K=12$
amplitudes per nerve.  Target fascicles were selected by a
peripheral-cluster selector that picks one contiguous angular
sector of the nerve perimeter containing the largest
peripheral-fascicle cluster (by total fibre count), giving every
nerve a single anatomically contiguous target region.
Figure~\ref{fig:duke}\textbf{a} shows, for a representative swine and
human nerve, the fibre activation maps and unrolled 12-contact cuff
patterns under dense- and sparse-optimised stimulation (the sparse
condition is defined in Section~\ref{sec:results-sparse}); fibres are
coloured by post-optimisation activation status
(target/off-target $\times$ fired/silent).

\paragraph{Dense optimisation recovers focal, selective drive.}
The rectangular optimiser uses a probe-based smart initialisation
(Section~\ref{sec:methods-opt}): a small set of bipolar and
single-column tripolar spatial patterns is scored at eight magnitudes
in 80 forward passes per seed, and the best initialises a single
gradient (Adam-FD) trajectory with the probe's zero contacts frozen to
preserve sparsity.  Across the cohort (116 seeds over 29 nerves) this
achieves mean $\mathrm{SI} = 0.90$ (median $0.95$), with $70\,\%$ of
seeds reaching the $\mathrm{SI} \geq 0.90$ noise-floor threshold.
Swine and human reach a near-identical median ($0.946$ vs.\ $0.965$),
but the human \emph{mean} is lower ($0.857$ vs.\ $0.926$) because a
heavy tail of geometric-ceiling anatomies (below) pulls it down rather
than a uniform downward shift.  The optimiser converges to a focal,
sparse drive: $105$ of $116$ seeds ($91\,\%$) use exactly three active
contacts --- a single-column tripolar configuration (cathode at the
column's $z$-middle contact, charge-balancing anodes at $z$-top and
$z$-bottom).  Per-sample dense and sparse-transfer activation maps with their cuff
patterns, for every nerve and seed, are provided as Supplementary
Figures~\ref{fig:supp-sub-4-sam-3}--\ref{fig:supp-human-sub-58-sam-3}.
All results here use optimised rectangular pulses; waveform-shape
optimisation is left to future work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Emergent stimulation strategy.}
The optimised configurations are physically interpretable rather than
arbitrary (Figure~\ref{fig:duke}\textbf{c}).  Circumferentially, the
optimiser steers cathodic drive \emph{toward} the target sector: the
fraction of contacts that are cathodic is highest in the
target-facing direction ($0.34$ swine, $0.40$ human) and near zero to
the side.  Longitudinally it forms a centre--surround current profile
--- a mid-cathode tripole flanked by charge-balancing anodes ---
whose polarity is a species-dependent tendency (more often the
classic mid-cathode form in swine, the inverse in human).  The
optimiser thus rediscovers, without being told to, the steered
tripolar configurations that the selectivity literature arrives at by
design.

\paragraph{Geometric-ceiling cases.}
The cohort separates into anatomies the cuff can steer and a minority
it cannot.  Geometric-ceiling cases have a target-to-off-target
minimum-distance ratio close to unity --- the off-target fascicles sit
as close to the contacts as the target --- so no per-contact
configuration has a spatial gradient to exploit, and the optimiser
either co-activates off-target fascicles or fails to reach threshold.
Four of the $116$ seeds (all human) fall below $\mathrm{SI} = 0.5$ and
are excluded from the dense-versus-sparse comparison below as they
provide no valid selectivity ceiling.  That gradient-based
optimisation succeeds on real cross-species microCT anatomies, and
honestly reports the geometric ceiling where it does not, is the
principal application result of this paper.

\subsection{Sparse fibre-sampling bias and the deployment penalty}
\label{sec:results-sparse}

Prior GPU-accelerated selectivity work reduces each fascicle to a
single centroid fibre to keep the population
tractable~\cite{hussain2024}.  Because jaxon simulates the full
${\sim}1000$-fibre population directly, we can ask what this
approximation costs at deployment.  For each nerve $\times$ seed we
re-ran the identical optimisation on four sparse fibre-sampling levels
--- one centroid fibre per fascicle, and $1$, $3$ or $10$
randomly-sampled fibres per fascicle --- by pure row-subsampling of the
precomputed $V_e$ lead-field with no FEM recomputation.  The
selectivity such a model \emph{reports} is optimistic, because its
amplitudes are tuned to the few fibres it can see; we therefore take
each sparse-optimised amplitude vector and re-score it on the full
population \emph{without re-optimising}, giving the transfer
selectivity $\mathrm{SI_{transfer}}$ --- the selectivity actually
delivered.  The deployment penalty is the drop from the dense ceiling,
$\mathrm{SI_{dense}} - \mathrm{SI_{transfer}}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:duke}\textbf{d}).

The penalty is small in swine but large in human.  Per nerve it is a
median $0.03$ in swine (mean $0.04$) versus $0.27$ in human (mean
$0.31$; Mann--Whitney $p = 10^{-4}$), and the transfer SI lies
significantly below the dense ceiling for every sampling strategy in
both species (two-sided per-nerve Wilcoxon, Holm-corrected; all
$p \le 0.008$).  Sampling \emph{density} barely matters: ten fibres per
fascicle transfer no better than one, or than the centroid, so the
shortfall is not closed by sampling a few more fibres.

What drives it is fascicle \emph{size}.  The per-nerve penalty scales
with the number of fibres per target fascicle (Spearman $r = +0.72$,
$p < 10^{-3}$, $n = 29$ nerves), which has median $30$ in swine versus
$92$ in human.  This mediates the species gap: swine vagus nerves carry
many small fascicles, so a handful of sampled fibres represents the
target well and the optimised field transfers; human nerves carry a few
large fascicles, so a field tuned to one sampled fibre does not cover
the rest of that fascicle.  Counterintuitively, the more fascicular
nerve (swine) is the more forgiving one.

The recruitment signature confirms the mechanism
(Figure~\ref{fig:duke}\textbf{b}).  Relative to the dense optimum, the
sparse-transfer configuration in human loses on-target recruitment
(median $100\,\% \rightarrow 65\,\%$; $p = 0.023$) and selectivity
($p = 0.014$), whereas off-target recruitment and total delivered
current are statistically unchanged ($p > 0.4$).  The penalty is thus a
failure to recruit the large target fascicle, not off-target spill or
reduced drive.  The practical implication is that centroid-only fibre
sampling --- standard in GPU-accelerated selectivity
work~\cite{hussain2024} --- recovers most of the achievable selectivity
on small-fascicle anatomies but materially over-states the selectivity
that will actually be delivered on large-fascicle nerves, precisely the
human vagus geometry most relevant to clinical translation.
